**It’s not about being a bad person. It’s about understanding what your brain is trying to cope with.**

You had a stressful day.

You feel lonely.

You’re anxious about money.

You had an argument with someone you love.

You’re overwhelmed.

And you reach for something that makes you feel better.

For some people, that’s food.

For others, it’s alcohol.

Some people scroll social media for hours.

Others gamble.

And for some people, sexual activity, pornography or masturbation becomes the quickest way to escape an uncomfortable emotional state.

There’s nothing inherently wrong with having sexual desire.

Sexual behaviour can be pleasurable, intimate and completely normal.

The question is:

**What role is it playing in your life?**

Are you choosing it because you genuinely want to?

Or are you increasingly using it because you don’t know what else to do with what you’re feeling?

That distinction matters.

### **1. You reach for it mainly when you feel bad**

Pay attention to the pattern.

You feel:

* stressed → porn
* lonely → masturbation
* rejected → sex
* anxious → sexual content
* emotionally numb → sexual activity

The behaviour temporarily changes how you feel.

That relief can teach your brain:

**“When I feel bad, this makes me feel better.”**

And the cycle can become stronger over time.

This doesn’t mean every time someone masturbates when stressed, they have a problem.

Coping behaviours are common.

The concern is when sexual behaviour becomes **one of your main or only ways of regulating difficult emotions.** 

Read our piece on [signs you need therapy but won’t admit it. ](https://mindmed.com.ng/blog/signs-you-need-therapy-nigeria-2026/)

### **2. You’ve tried to change the behaviour but repeatedly struggle to**

Maybe you’ve told yourself:

**“I’m going to stop watching porn.”**

Or:

**“I’m going to cut down.”**

Or:

**“I’m not going to use sex to deal with stress anymore.”**

And then you find yourself doing it again.

Trying to change a behaviour and struggling doesn’t automatically mean you’re addicted.

But repeated difficulty controlling a behaviour, especially when you’ve made multiple attempts to change it, can be worth exploring.

The important question isn’t:

**“How many times do you do it?”**

It’s:

**“Do you feel in control of your choices?”**

### **3. It’s starting to interfere with your life**

This is where the situation becomes more concerning.

Maybe you’re:

* staying awake late watching porn and becoming exhausted 
* missing work or important responsibilities spending money you can’t afford on sexual content or encounters
* avoiding your partner 
* neglecting friendships 
* cancelling plans repeatedly
* struggling to concentrate
* continuing despite serious consequences

Your sexual behaviour doesn’t have to be frequent to become a problem.

What matters is **the impact it’s having on your life and your ability to control it.** 




### **4. You need more stimulation to get the same relief**

Sometimes coping behaviour changes over time.

What once gave you a quick sense of relief may stop working as strongly.

You may start spending longer doing it.

Seeking more intense or novel material.

Repeating the behaviour more frequently.

Or needing increasingly specific stimulation to experience the same effect.

Again, this doesn’t automatically mean you have a disorder.

But if you’re noticing that the behaviour is escalating **and you don’t feel fully in control of the escalation**, it’s worth paying attention to.




### **5. The shame afterward is becoming part of the cycle**

This one can be particularly complicated in Nigeria.

For some people, sexual behaviour is surrounded by strong religious, cultural or moral expectations.

So the cycle can look like:

**Stress → sexual behaviour → temporary relief → guilt/shame → more distress → sexual behaviour again.**

And now the behaviour isn’t just something you’re doing.

It’s become part of a cycle you’re struggling to escape.

Shame can make it harder to seek help because you may think:

**“What kind of person does this make me?”**

But mental-health support isn’t about judging your morality.

It’s about understanding your behaviour, your emotions and what you actually need.




## **So, Is Sexual Behaviour Automatically a Problem?**

**No.**

Having a high sex drive doesn’t automatically mean something is wrong.

Masturbation doesn’t automatically mean addiction.

Watching porn doesn’t automatically mean you have a mental-health disorder.

And having sex frequently doesn’t automatically mean you’re using sex to cope.

What matters is the **pattern**.

Are you experiencing significant distress?

Do you feel unable to control the behaviour?

Is it interfering with your relationships, work, finances or daily life?

Are you repeatedly trying to change it and struggling?

Those are more useful questions than simply asking:

**“How often do you do it?”**

The World Health Organization’s ICD-11 includes **Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder (CSBD)** under impulse-control disorders. The focus isn’t simply on sexual desire; clinically relevant concerns involve persistent difficulty controlling the behaviour alongside significant distress or impairment.

## **What Can You Do If You Recognize Yourself Here?**

Start with curiosity rather than punishment.

For one week, notice what happens **before** the behaviour.

Ask yourself:

**What was I feeling?**

**What happened immediately before?**

**What was I hoping the behaviour would make me feel?**

**Did I actually feel better afterward?**

**What did I need in that moment that I wasn’t getting?**

Maybe it was connection.

Maybe rest.

Maybe reassurance.

Maybe excitement.

Maybe emotional escape.

Maybe simply a break from an overwhelming day.

The goal isn’t necessarily to shame yourself into stopping.

It’s to understand the cycle.

And once you understand the cycle, you can begin building **other ways of coping**.




## You Don’t Have to Talk About This Alone

Talking about sexual behaviour can feel incredibly uncomfortable, especially when shame, religion, relationships or cultural expectations are involved.

But therapy isn’t a courtroom.

You don’t have to walk in and defend yourself.

You can simply say:

**“I’ve noticed that I use sexual behaviour when I’m stressed, and I’m not sure I know how to stop.”**

That’s enough of a starting point.

**You don’t have to be ashamed to ask for help.**
