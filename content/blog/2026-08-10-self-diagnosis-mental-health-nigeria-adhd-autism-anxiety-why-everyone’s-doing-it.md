“I think I have ADHD.”

“I’m pretty sure I’m autistic.”

“I watched three TikToks and I have anxiety and BPD.”

If you came here after Googling your symptoms at 2am, welcome. You’re not crazy. You’re not attention-seeking.

A lot of the people who reach out to us already have a word for how they feel before we’ve even spoken. Some say “I diagnosed myself with ADHD.” Others say “I think I’m on the spectrum.”

Let’s talk about why that’s happening, and what to do next. No shame.

## “I Diagnosed Myself On TikTok”: You’re Not Alone

First, you did the right thing by looking for answers.

For years in Nigeria, if you felt different, you were told to pray about it, or that you were just lazy, or to stop overthinking. There was no language for it.

Now TikTok, Reddit, Twitter, and YouTube are giving people that language for the first time. That’s why I can’t focus unless it’s urgent. That’s why social situations drain me for days. That’s why my brain feels like fifty tabs are open.

That feeling of finally, someone gets me, is real. It’s validating. Don’t let anyone shame you for finding comfort in that.

## Why Are So Many Nigerians Self-Diagnosing In 2026?

It’s not just a trend. A few things are colliding at once.

**Access to information.** For the first time, Nigerians can see creators explaining ADHD, autism, and anxiety in Pidgin, in Yoruba, through stories that actually sound like real life, not textbook English from decades ago. When you see yourself in a video, it clicks.

**Burnout culture.** Long work hours. No leave. Economic stress. Doing three jobs to survive. That constant overwhelm looks a lot like ADHD: forgetfulness, procrastination, starting ten things and finishing none. Chronic stress looks a lot like anxiety: racing thoughts, can’t sleep, always on edge.

**Doctors who dismiss people.** Many people told a doctor “I feel this way” and heard “you’re fine,” or “it’s spiritual,” or “just rest.” So they went home and started researching on their own. Can you blame them?

**The need for answers.** When you’ve felt off your whole life, finding a label feels like relief. Even if it’s not fully accurate yet, it gives you direction, and it gives you hope that it isn’t a character flaw.

## When Self-Diagnosis Helps, And When It Hurts

Let’s be fair to both sides.

It helps when it gives you language for what you feel, when it connects you to people who relate, on TikTok or in a group chat, and when it becomes the first real step toward getting support. Most people don’t book therapy until they’ve Googled first.

It hurts when it leads somewhere wrong. Burnout can look like ADHD. Trauma can look like autism. Depression can look like laziness. A thyroid issue can look like brain fog. If you treat anxiety like ADHD, the coping strategies won’t fit, and you’ll feel worse instead of better. You might also miss the actual issue entirely, grief, sleep problems, or workplace stress that needs a completely different approach.

Google and ChatGPT can hand you possible answers. What they can’t do is rule things out, or see your full history. That’s where a professional comes in.

## ADHD vs Anxiety vs Autism vs Burnout: Why They Feel The Same

This is exactly why it’s so confusing, and why so many people relate to everything they see online.

Can’t focus, keep procrastinating, forget things? That could be ADHD. It could also be anxiety, depression, or plain burnout from working too much.

Social situations leave you drained for days? That could be autism. It could also be social anxiety, or depression pulling you to withdraw.

Overthinking everything, can’t switch your brain off? That could be anxiety. It could also be ADHD, or OCD.

Shutting down under stress, going quiet? That could be autism. It could also be trauma, or severe burnout.

Your brain is trying to tell you something. But the label isn’t always obvious from a sixty-second video. That’s why getting properly assessed matters, not to prove you wrong, but to get you the right kind of help. ## Three Steps If You Think You Have ADHD, Anxiety, Or Autism

**Take yourself seriously.** Your struggle is real whether or not it has a name yet. Don’t minimize it with “maybe I’m just lazy.” You felt something. Honor that.

**Track your symptoms for two weeks.** When does it happen? What triggers it? What makes it better? How is it showing up at work, school, or in your relationships? This helps a therapist enormously, and it helps you see your own patterns too.

**Get a proper assessment.** A therapist won’t just say “yes, you have ADHD” after five minutes. They’ll ask about your childhood, your current stress, your sleep, your medical history. They’ll use real screening tools and rule out other explanations. In Nigeria, you can now do this online and privately, without walking into a hospital and explaining yourself to five different people.
