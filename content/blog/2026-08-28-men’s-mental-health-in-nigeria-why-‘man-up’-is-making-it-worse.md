“Man up.”

“Shed no tears.”

“You’re the man of the house now.”

If you’re a Nigerian man, you’ve heard some version of this since you were a boy. Maybe you said it to your own son last week without even thinking.

Nobody sat you down and taught you what to do with pain. They just taught you to hide it.

## Why Nigerian Men Don’t Talk About It

It’s not that men don’t struggle. It’s that struggling was never made safe.

**You were raised to provide, not to feel.** From a young age, a man’s worth got tied to what he could carry: school fees, rent, his mother’s medical bills, his siblings’ futures. Feelings weren’t part of the job description. If anything, having them felt like a distraction from the actual work of being a man.

**Vulnerability reads as weakness.** Admitting you’re struggling can feel like admitting you’re failing at the one job you were raised for. So instead of “I’m not okay,” it becomes “I’m just tired,” or nothing at all.

**Male friendships rarely go deep.** Many men have people to laugh with, watch football with, do business with. Fewer have someone they can say “I don’t know what I’m doing anymore” to without it changing how that person sees them.

**Therapy still sounds like something for “mad people” or for women.** Even now, plenty of men quietly believe that going to see someone about your feelings is a sign something is seriously wrong with you, not a normal, reasonable thing to do.

Put all of that together, and you get a lot of men carrying a lot, alone, with nowhere it’s supposed to go.

## How It Actually Shows Up

Struggling doesn’t always look like sadness. In men, it often shows up sideways.

1.	**Irritability and anger:** Snapping at your wife, your kids, your staff, over things that didn’t used to bother you

2.	**Overworking:** Using the office as an escape from everything else, staying “busy” so you don’t have to feel

3.	**Drinking more than usual:** Using alcohol to switch off at the end of the day, and needing more of it over time

4.	**Withdrawing:** Going quiet, skipping hangouts, feeling like you have nothing left to give anyone

5.	**Risk-taking:** Reckless spending, reckless driving, affairs, things that create a rush or a distraction

6.	**Physical complaints:** Chest tightness, headaches, stomach issues, sleep problems, with no clear medical cause

If you recognized yourself in more than one of these, that’s worth sitting with, not brushing off.

## Why “Man Up” Makes It Worse

“Man up” doesn’t remove the pain. It just removes your permission to look at it.

Suppressed pain doesn’t disappear, it leaks out somewhere else: into your marriage, into how you parent, into your health, into the wall you keep between yourself and everyone around you. Our piece on [when love hurts in relationships](https://mindmed.com.ng/blog/relationships-mental-health-nigeria/) explores how this often lands hardest on the people closest to you, sometimes without either of you realizing why.

The men who eventually do get support usually say the same thing afterward: they wish someone had told them sooner that carrying it alone was never actually strength.

## What Actually Helps

**Name it, even just to yourself.** You don’t have to announce it to anyone. Just stop calling it “stress” if it’s actually something heavier. Being honest with yourself first is the real first step.

**Find one person you can be fully honest with.** Not everyone. One. A brother, a close friend, a partner, someone you trust not to see you differently for it.

**Let your body move.** Exercise won’t fix everything, but it genuinely helps process the tension that builds up when feelings have nowhere else to go.

**Try therapy, even if it feels strange at first.** It’s not about being broken. It’s a private space where you don’t have to perform strength for anyone, including yourself. If you’ve been putting it off, our guide on the [signs you might need therapy](https://mindmed.com.ng/blog/signs-you-need-therapy-nigeria-2026/) is worth a look.

**Watch the coping habits**. If alcohol, overworking, or withdrawal have quietly become your main way of managing stress, that’s worth addressing directly, not just managing around.

## When To See A Therapist

Consider booking a session if:

•	You’ve been more irritable or angry than usual for weeks, and can’t quite say why

•	You’re relying on alcohol, work, or distraction just to get through the day

•	People close to you have said you seem different, and part of you knows they’re right

•	You feel like you have to hold everything together, with no space to fall apart, even briefly
